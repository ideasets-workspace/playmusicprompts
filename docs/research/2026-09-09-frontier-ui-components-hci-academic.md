# Findings per angle, with five-part records for every academic [FULL] source

## Angle (a) — Target size and pointing

### Five-part record A16 — Parhi, Karlson & Bederson, MobileHCI 2006 [FULL]
1. **Problem (authors' framing):** target-size guidance existed for stylus-on-PDA and index-finger-on-desktop, "but never for thumbs when holding a small device in a single hand"; widgets compete for screen space so targets should be "as small as possible without degrading performance or user satisfaction".
2. **Method:** N=20 (17 M, 3 F, 19–42 y, right-handed), standing, right thumb, HP iPAQ h4155 (240×320, 0.24 mm dot pitch), lift-off selection, 0 mm spacing, distractor targets. Phase 1 discrete: 5 sizes (3.8, 5.8, 7.7, 9.6, 11.5 mm) × 9 screen regions × 5 reps = 225 trials; start button N/S of target at constant distance. Phase 2 serial: 5 key sizes (5.8–13.4 mm) × 4 regions × 5 reps, 4-digit codes on a soft keypad. RM-ANOVA with Greenhouse–Geisser; Fitts' law MT = a + b·log2(A/W+1) as the interpretive frame.
3. **Results:** discrete task time main effect of size F(1,25)=70.42, p<.001 (all pairwise significant, even 9.6 vs 11.5 mm p=.04); discrete error main effect F(1,27)=49.18, p<.001, **no difference between 9.6 and 11.5 mm**; 2-SD hit boxes 6.5×6.5 mm (3.8 mm target) → 9.1×8.9 mm (9.6 mm target), i.e. 95 % of hits at any location fit in 9.1×8.9 mm; no effect of screen location on time or error. Serial: time F(1,25)=60.02, p<.001 (all sizes differ; Fitts predicts equality because keypads scaled uniformly — authors attribute to thumb–key size interaction); error F(2,43)=11.83, p<.001, 5.8 mm worse than ≥9.6 mm, others n.s.; size×location F(12,228)=1.87, p=.039. Comfort: centre 5.7/7, NW/SW 3.7/7; smallest comfortable size 6.0 mm centre, 7.5–7.7 mm corners; right-column hits skew right. Recommendation: ≥9.2 mm discrete, ≥9.6 mm serial.
4. **Stated limitations:** standing not walking; PDA grip may differ from phones; small ID range unsuitable for reporting a, b. Analyst-identified: 2006 device (8.9 cm diagonal) versus 2026 phones of 15–17 cm — reach findings should be read qualitatively; 3 women of 20; lift-off selection differs from modern touch-down/tap semantics.
5. **Application here:** transport controls (play, skip, like, queue) and the six radio tiles are discrete, frequently used, often one-handed → the evidence-backed size is the 9.2 mm class (≈58 CSS px on a 160-px/in phone — CALCULATION), not the 24 px legal floor; place primary controls in the centre/lower-centre band, avoid NW/SW corners for one-thumb actions, and let right-edge targets bleed to the bezel for right-hand use (mirror for RTL/left-hand setting is OWNER-DECISION).

### Five-part record A01 — Ko, Zhao, Ramakrishnan, Zhai & Bi, Chinese CHI 2021 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** Finger-Fitts law (Bi, Li & Zhai 2013) models the "fat finger" absolute-precision term with the effective width We and a pre-calibrated σa; should it use the nominal width W (the designer's number) and a free parameter c instead?
2. **Method:** six candidate models: Fitts-W, Fitts-We, FF-We-σa, FF-W-σa, FF-We-c, FF-W-c where FF-W-c is MT = a + b·log2(A/√(W²−c²)+1). Exp 1: N=23, Pixel C tablet (308 PPI), horizontal bars W ∈ {2,4,8,12,20} mm × A ∈ {24,48,80} mm, 3,450 successful trials; Exp 2: N=14, circles W ∈ {4,6,8,10,12} mm × A ∈ {16,28,60} mm, 7,980 trials; σa = 0.94 mm (bars) / 1.5 mm (circles) from Bi et al.; leave-one-(A,W)-out RMSE, AIC/WAIC/BIC; re-analysis of the 2013 dataset.
3. **Results:** Table 1 (bars): MT 0.63/0.54/0.46/0.42/0.38 s and error 39.3 %/18.1 %/4.8 %/2.1 %/0.0 % for 2/4/8/12/20 mm; width F(4,88)=158.4, p<.001 on MT and F(4,88)=122.5, p<.001 on error; distance did not affect error (F(2,44)=2.31, p=.111). Table 5 (circles): error 24.9 %/10.9 %/6.4 %/2.8 %/1.1 % for 4/6/8/10/12 mm. Model fitness: FF-W-c R²=0.984 vs Fitts-W 0.980 vs Fitts-We 0.720; free-parameter c always beat pre-defined σa after AIC/WAIC/BIC penalties.
4. **Stated limitations:** two shapes, one tablet, seated lab task; nominal vs effective width choice is dataset-dependent. Analyst: index-finger tablet pointing, not one-handed thumb; young sample (21–36).
5. **Application here:** gives the design-time predictor the rebuild can use without an endpoint study — plug nominal CSS sizes into FF-W-c to compare candidate control sizes; the error curve (≈5 % at 8 mm, ≈2 % at 12 mm) is the empirical basis for choosing between the 24-px legal floor and a ≥8–10 mm (≈50–63 CSS px) practical floor for prompt-field chips and queue-row actions.

### Standards chain (S01, S06, S02, S03) — why 24 × 24 CSS px
- SC 2.5.8 text and its five exceptions (Spacing, Equivalent, Inline, User-agent control, Essential) are quoted in S01; a solid 24×24 CSS px axis-aligned square must fit inside the target; the 24 px spacing circle is centred on the target's bounding box; the rule is zoom-independent and does not apply to targets obscured by transient content; **the only Related Resource listed by W3C is Parhi et al. 2006**.
- S06 shows the Mobile A11y TF's original text: **48 px for coarse pointers, 24 px for fine pointers, Level A**, evidence list = Apple 44 pt, Android 48 dp, Microsoft 26–44 px, fingertip width 16–20 mm, Parhi 9.2/9.6 mm. S03/S02 record the WG's final position: 24 px AA as "a workable compromise between extant recommendations of larger sizes … and the needs of interfaces where a lot of icon-based controls need to be simultaneously available", with 44 px kept at AAA (SC 2.5.5); issue #1456: "24px was selected as a combination of the research document, with an error margin of 15%".
- CALCULATION (inputs: S01 glossary "CSS pixel = visual angle of about 0.0213 degrees"; CSS reference pixel 1/96 in): 24 px ≈ 0.51°; at the reference pixel 24 px = 6.35 mm and 44 px = 11.64 mm; on a phone at 160 CSS px/in (1 px = 0.159 mm) 24 px = 3.8 mm, 44 px = 7.0 mm, 48 px = 7.6 mm; Parhi's 9.2/9.6 mm ≈ 58/60 CSS px; Ko's 8 mm (4.8 % error) ≈ 50 CSS px, 12 mm (2.1 %) ≈ 76 CSS px. Consequence (INFERENCE): 24 px satisfies the law but sits far below every empirical thumb-error plateau; 44/48 px sits at ~7–7.6 mm, still under Parhi's plateau but at Ko's ≈5 % error band.

### Supporting [ABS] evidence
- A32 Ergonomics 2022 (N=104, 57 M/47 F): one-thumb clicks "partially conformed to Fitts' Law"; MT rose as icon size fell but **not above 110 rpx**; users rated 140 rpx best; MT rose with vertical distance; same-side targets slower; men faster than women.
- A08 Karlson HCIL 2006: device size does not slow reachable targets but larger devices have more unreachable area; **NW↔SE diagonal thumb movement is hardest** for right-handers; corner slow-downs 7–12 %.
- A09 Park & Han: 4/7/10 mm keys × 25 locations, N=40; success rate rises with size; accurate regions differ by size; offsets ~Gaussian.
- A28 FFitts law CHI 2013 (ABS): dual-distribution hypothesis; R² ≥ 0.91 across 1D, 2D and typing.

## Angle (b) — Dark UI legibility, polarity, contrast method

### Five-part record A17 — Dobres, Chahine & Reimer, Applied Ergonomics 2017 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** the "positive polarity advantage" was established with long-form, self-paced tasks in dim rooms (5–550 lx); unknown whether it holds for glance reading and under bright ambient light, and whether it is really a luminance/pupil effect.
2. **Method:** 1-interval forced-choice lexical decision (Italian words/pseudowords, Neue Helvetica 55, 0.7 m, 17" LCD), 3-down/1-up adaptive staircase converging on 79.4 % accuracy; threshold = median display time of last 20 trials; 2 polarities × 2 cap-heights (3 mm ≈ 14.7′, 4 mm ≈ 19.6′) × 2 ambient (≈0 lx vs 4750 lx); 100 trials/condition; N=34 analysed (13 F; 50 recruited, 16 excluded by pre-set criteria); 2×2×2 RM-ANOVA.
3. **Results (Table 2, ms):** day: neg 88.7/92.2, pos 86.0/77.5 (3/4 mm); night: **neg 122.3/106.9, pos 84.1/83.3**. Main effects lighting F(1,33)=7.61 p=.009; polarity F=24.40 p<.001; size F=4.21 p=.048; lighting×polarity F=14.18 p<.001. Bright: only size significant (F=5.49, p=.025; 87.4 vs 84.8 ms), polarity n.s. (F=0.19, p=.665). Dark: polarity F=49.60 p<.001; size mattered only for negative polarity (t(33)=2.31, p=.027). Accuracy converged at 79.1 % in all cells (Table 1).
4. **Stated limitations:** high-functioning FCA employees; ambient order not counterbalanced (bright first) so a practice effect may have lowered night thresholds; Italian only. Analyst: single typeface, pure #000/#FFF only (no dark-grey surfaces tested), LCD not OLED.
5. **Application here:** the project is dark by decision and used worldwide, often at night. The evidence says the cost of dark is concentrated where the product will live (dim rooms) and on SMALL text; the mitigations the paper licenses are **larger text on the dark surface** and awareness that a 3 → 4 mm cap-height change (≈+33 %) removed the size penalty even in negative polarity. Feeds the type-scale lower bound and the "no dense small reading on the darkest surface" constraint already in the 2026-08-29 report.

### Five-part record A03 — Palmén, Gilbert & Crossland (Google), CHI 2023 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** does the variable-font **grade** axis (boldness without reflow) recover readability lost in dark mode via halation and luminance, for glance and paragraph reading?
2. **Method:** Study 1 (MTurk, 184 → 126 after Levenshtein-based cleaning): 56 word pairs, Roboto Flex 16 px/500/0.15 px tracking, 5 grades (−200…150) × 2 modes, space-bar timed; power analysis n≥126 for .85. Study 2 (Cint US panel, 410 → 333): two ~105-word paragraphs on Android phones, Roboto Flex 14 px/400/0.25 px, #121212/#FFFFFF, 6 grades, 2 comprehension MCQs each, Qualtrics timing; repeated-measures ANOVAs.
3. **Results:** Study 1: MODE F(1,106)=8.578, p=.004, η²=.075; GRADE F=1.855 p=.117; MODE×GRADE F=2.406 p=.048; at grade 62.5 DM 2.06 s vs LM 1.93 s; grade ≥62.5 helped LM only. Study 2: MODE F=12.8, p<.01, η²=.038, t(332)=3.5; **no grade effect, no interaction**; age between-groups F=3.0 p=.007; interest slowed reading (LM F=2.5 p=.04; DM F=4.7 p<.001). Preferences: phone setting 34 % LM / 36 % DM / 25 % switch; "visually clearer" 41 % LM / 42 % DM / 17 % neither; preferred mode 33/33/34 %. 68 % of adults read 3.0–4.5 words/s at 14 px.
4. **Stated limitations:** one typeface, Material letter-spacing only (tighter tracking could reverse the grade result), no scrolling, crowdsourced, luminance not equalised across modes. Analyst: grade range asymmetric to weight; effect sizes small (η² .038–.075); no OLED/ambient control.
5. **Application here:** the rebuild plans variable-font axes; this is the only controlled evidence on an axis × polarity interaction. It says (i) LM/DM fluency gap persists at 14–16 px even with a well-tuned variable font, (ii) pushing grade/weight up in DM is NOT a measured fix, so it must be justified aesthetically not as an accessibility remedy, (iii) users' mode preference is split three ways and does not track their own performance — a user-selectable theme is the behaviour the data support, which the owner has already declined; record, do not relitigate.

### Contradiction preserved (C1): studies that favour negative polarity

Both are recorded as facts the dark build must design around (size, luminance, user control of brightness).

## Angle (c) — Typography: size floors, line length, variable fonts

### Five-part record A23 — Atılgan, Xiong & Legge, PNAS 2020 [FULL] (SI appendices not read)
1. **Problem:** print size and display size jointly limit how many characters fit on a line; nobody had modelled their joint effect on reading speed for normal and low vision on laptop/tablet/phone.
2. **Method:** N=30 normally sighted (Times n=14, Courier n=16; 60 cm) + 10 low-vision (Times; 40 cm); Grimms' stories at 8 print sizes × 3 displays (24 conditions), plus a diffusing-goggle condition (acuity 0.83 logMAR). Models: Eq. 1 exponential drop of log reading speed with print size (CPS); Eq. 2 same form for character count (CCC = smallest count giving 80 % of MRS); Eq. 3 joint model with count expressed as a function of angular size for a given display/font.
3. **Results (Table 1):** no-blur CCC 12.8 [10.5,15.0] laptop-Times … 13.6 phone-Courier; **average 12.8 characters**; font n.s. (F(1,28)=3.74, P=.06); only tablet-vs-laptop Courier differed (MD 3.1, P=.005). MRS 300–306 wpm Times, 285–298 Courier. Blur: MRS 260/227 wpm; CCC 9.2 laptop/tablet, 16.4 phone (likely simulation artefact); recommended-size range 0.29 logMAR laptop, 0.20 tablet, **none on the phone** (range −0.15/−0.28 logMAR) because CPS > size-at-CCC. Low vision: CCC 8.4 mean (7.3 tablet–9.1 laptop); MRS 30–398 wpm; CPS 0.47–1.84 logMAR. Table 3: a 10-cm-wide phone serves acuity up to 0.97 logMAR at 40 cm, 1.41 at 15 cm.
4. **Stated limitations:** simulated blur ≠ low vision (phone CCC artefact); English only; two fonts. Analyst: perceptual-span explanation is inferential; 60/40 cm fixed distances; no dark polarity condition.
5. **Application here:** every text container on phone width (chips, queue rows, radio names, prompt tokens, lyric lines) must keep **≥ 13 characters per line at the chosen size**, otherwise the reader is pushed below 80 % of their reading speed even if the type is "large enough"; the CPS ≈ 0.2° x-height (Legge & Bigelow) sets the lower size bound. CALCULATION at 30 cm phone distance: 0.2° = 1.05 mm x-height ≈ 6.6 CSS px x-height at 160 px/in → for a font with x-height ratio 0.52 that is ≈ 12.7 px font-size as the *acuity* floor (fluency, not comfort); the 2026-08-29 report's halation/size advice pushes body text well above this on dark.

### Line length (A10 [ABS], A11 [PARTIAL], A12 [ABS], WCAG 1.4.8)
- Dyson & Haselgrove 2001: 55 cpl gave the highest comprehension and was read faster than short lines; 100 cpl disrupted return sweeps (the authors note an untested optimum may lie between 55 and 100).
- A11 (Cambridge review, Table 1): 55 cpl preferred (Dyson & Haselgrove 2001; McMullin 2002) vs 85–100 cpl recommended by Dyson & Kipping 1998 and Shaikh & Chaparro 2005a/b; 1.5 line spacing improved efficiency and accuracy in several studies.
- A12 (2025 SLR): "ideal line lengths of 50–70 characters (10–14 words) align with the natural perceptual span"; critical spacing improves reading, over-spacing gives no gain.
- WCAG SC 1.4.8 (AAA): ≤ 80 characters (40 CJK) — normative at AAA only.
Status: "moderate line length, extremes hurt" is 3+ verified; the single optimum is CONTESTED (55 vs 85–100) — record as a range 50–75 cpl with 80 as the AAA ceiling; exact `ch` value is OWNER-DECISION.

### Font individuation and variable axes (A13 [PARTIAL], A30 [ABS])
- Wallace et al. TOCHI 2022: 16 fonts perceptually size-normalised to Times 16 px; N=352 (18–71 y); **35 % WPM gain fastest vs slowest font per individual**, comprehension ≈ 90 % unchanged; preference does not predict speed; "one font does not fit all". `[single-source]` (one study family; Adobe/Brown/UCF).
- Guidi et al. (JoV VSS 2023/2024/2025): Roboto Flex axes — slant produced a 38.4 % WPM change across its range (WPM up as slant → 0), thick stroke smallest effect (22.7 %), thin stroke fastest at minimum; comprehension stable (81.5 %); sentence-duration thresholds U-shaped for thin/thick stroke; extreme thin strokes slowest regardless of width; readers lengthen saccades to compensate for wider text. `[ABS]` ×3, same lab → `[single-family]`.
- Palmén 2023 (above): grade has no measurable DM effect at 14 px.
KNOWLEDGE: variable-font axes are measurable readability levers (slant, stroke contrast, width) whose extremes cost speed; the rebuild's optical-size/width/grade choices are OWNER-DECISION inside the bound "avoid axis extremes for body text".

### `text-wrap: balance` and line-break position
NOT FOUND IN THE SEARCHED SCOPE (Q26): no controlled study of CSS balanced wrapping. Adjacent evidence: Parker et al. 2023 (N=39, eye-tracking) found no reliable passage-level effect of placing low-frequency words line-initial vs line-final; Tilburg thesis (bigram-frequency breaks, small displays) found no effect on speed/comprehension; subtitling study found viewers prefer syntactic breaks. INFERENCE: balance is a typographic-quality/aesthetic tool with negligible measured readability cost or gain; use for headings (MDN/Chrome limit it to ≤6/≤10 lines).

## Angle (d) — Motion, vection, vestibular safety

### Five-part record A07 — Peripheral Teleportation, IEEE TVCG 2025 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** continuous virtual locomotion causes cybersickness; black FOV restrictors are the common mitigation but cost visibility; rest-frame (Earth-referent) techniques were thought weak.
2. **Method:** two extra stereo camera rigs (RF0/RF1) render a periphery whose optical flow is coupled to the user's physical head motion; RF1 is teleported ahead every T=1 s and dissolved in with dithering between r_in=55° and r_out=60°; asymmetric lateral restrictor shifted 17° into turns. Between-subjects N=90 (45 F, 19–27 y, M=23.57), Meta Quest 2 (97° FOV, 72 Hz), city navigation, 10 trials × 2.5 min, 35 turns per trial; measures SSQ pre/post, per-trial discomfort 0–10 (ADS/RDS), task duration, visibility, IPQ presence; ANOVA / Kruskal–Wallis + Conover-Holm, Cohen's d / Cliff's δ.
3. **Results:** ΔSSQ n.s. (Control 69.07, Restrictor 68.07, PT 61.34; F(2)=0.37, p=.69). **ADS: PT 2.89 (SD 2.26) vs Restrictor 4.63 vs Control 4.53; F(2)=6.18, p=.004, η²=.130; PT vs Restrictor d=.83, PT vs Control d=.78; Restrictor≈Control d=.05.** RDS χ²(2)=22.18, p<.001; PT Mdn 5.80 vs Control 8.85 (δ=.64) vs Restrictor 7.83 (δ=.55). Task duration χ²(2)=17.52, p<.001; **PT Mdn 1215 s vs Restrictor 609.5 s vs Control 371 s**. Visibility and presence n.s.
4. **Stated limitations:** young campus sample; one outdoor urban VE; game-experience differences unexplored. Analyst: HMD-only — transfer to a monoscopic browser canvas (THE WORLD) is an inference; SSQ insensitive because of early stopping.
5. **Application here:** THE WORLD is a camera journey (continuous virtual locomotion, the highest-risk pattern). Transferable mechanisms: keep a stable, physically-coupled frame at the periphery (fixed HUD/chrome, static edges), prefer discrete/teleport station changes over long continuous dollies, make turns asymmetric-restricted or snap, and show that black vignetting alone did NOT beat control here. The reduced-motion alternative must be a *designed static composition*, not just a slower dolly.

### Vection drivers (A14 [PARTIAL], A36 [ABS], A15 [ABS])
- Hughes, Naeem & Davidenko 2024 (Exp 1 N=52, Exp 2 N=36; 60-s passive VR laps): speed raised vection (F(1,51)=71.98, p<.01, ηp²=.59) and sickness (F(1,51)=11.28, p<.01, ηp²=.18); expanding (forward) flow > contracting > lateral for vection (F(2,70)=5.05) and sickness (F(2,70)=6.65, p<.01); 30° reclined posture did not mitigate; vection and presence r=0.89–0.92, sickness uncorrelated with either.
- bioRxiv 2024 (five psychophysics experiments): segmenting the visual field with thin masks INCREASED VIMS; masking the central region reduced it — independent of motion pattern.
- CHI 2023 rotating-chair reading study (N=19, 76 sessions): visual motion cues congruent with real motion reduced symptoms most; sickness still reached problematic levels.
KNOWLEDGE for the audio-reactive scene: the sickness lever is optical-flow SPEED and forward-expanding flow filling the field; slowing the dolly, avoiding full-field zoom-in on beat, and keeping large stable regions are the evidence-backed controls. Web-specific pixel budgets circulating in practitioner guides ("≤ 8 px decorative translation", "speed ratio ≥ 0.85", "≤ 24 px interactive") were found only on vendor/SEO sites (Q6/Q15) → **[UNVERIFIED]**; do not cite them as research.

### Normative hooks and durations
- WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA) and 2.2.2 Pause/Stop/Hide (A) — `prefers-reduced-motion` is the implementation route; web.dev (Google) names parallax, zoom, autoplay video and scroll-linked layers as the triggers and describes vestibular reactions "dizziness, nausea, migraine … sometimes require bed rest".
- Duration numbers (NN/g: 100–500 ms envelope, ~100 ms toggles, 200–300 ms modal-scale, >500 ms feels like waiting, exits shorter than entrances; Val Head 200–500 ms; Material 150–200/300–400 ms via ux.stackexchange quote; perceptual cycle ≈ 230 ms, 70–700 ms from the Model Human Processor) are **practitioner consensus with a cognitive-model root, not experimental UI results** — no peer-reviewed duration study surfaced in Q16 → `[UNVERIFIED-academic]`. Motion tokens are OWNER-DECISION inside this envelope.

## Angle (e) — Custom vs native controls, ARIA, keyboard

### WebAIM Million 2025 (S04 [FULL]; data February 2025, WAVE on rendered DOM)
- 50,960,288 errors on 1,000,000 home pages = 51/page (−10.3 % vs 56.8 in 2024); 94.8 % of pages had detectable WCAG 2 failures; **low-contrast text on 79.1 % of pages, 29.6 instances/page** — the #1 issue; elements/page 1,257 (+7.1 %).
- ARIA: 105,509,338 attributes (~106/page, +18.5 % YoY, ~5× 2019); 79.4 % of pages use ARIA; **pages with ARIA 57 errors vs 27 without**; `role="menu"` on 4.5 % of pages and **35 % of those broken**; `aria-hidden="true"` 18/page; `tabindex=0/-1` 26/page; skip link on 15.3 % (1 in 10 broken); headings skipped on 39 % of pages.
- Frameworks: React pages 42.4 errors (−16.8 % vs mean), Vue 58.7 (+15.3 %), Angular 70.7 (+38.8 %); Bootstrap +12.2 errors. WebAIM's own caveat: correlation with complexity, not proof of causation; automated detection is a subset.

### Keyboard interaction cost (A18 [PARTIAL], ICSME 2026)
- 216 Tranco-top sites (Cochran sample, 95 %/5 %), keyboard crawler "Krawler" vs Crawljax/Qualstate mouse crawlers, 9 OS×browser configs (1,944 runs).
- RQ1: 73 % of sites need < 140 keystrokes for a full-page Tab traversal; 25 % need more (3.6–30 min at 1,500 ms/keystroke); 2 % untraversable.
- RQ2: **mean 50 keystrokes vs 10 clicks per use case (≈ 75 s vs 11 s)**; extreme 491 keystrokes (+3,992 %) caused by 83 tabbable items in a CLOSED menu and a broken skip link; 30 use cases uncompletable by keyboard; dense UIs (avg 615 elements) drove the gap; Instagram ≈ 200 Tabs to comment vs 2 clicks.
- RQ3: of UI states reachable by both modalities, **90 % were produced by standard HTML controls** (built-in keyboard + mouse handling); anti-patterns: hijacked key events, focus-trapping groups (carousels), infinite scroll without escape, non-focusable icon "buttons"; patterns: skip links, automatic focus shifts to modals, standard controls.
Status: `[single-source]` for the exact numbers (one team, preprint of an accepted ICSME 2026 paper); direction corroborated by S04 and by TetraLogical/LevelAccess practitioner sources.

### Screen-reader / custom-widget peer-reviewed evidence
- W4A 2015 "Look Ma, no ARIA" (ABS): ARIA "often used incorrectly and inconsistently, and sometimes even missing", 18 blind users study of generic widget interfaces.
- CHI 2024 Zong et al. (A26, ABS): 13 BLV participants; a **command box** for ephemeral customisation plus a settings menu; customisation eased identification/recall but "introduces additional complexity, making it more helpful for users familiar with similar tools".
- NOT FOUND IN THE SEARCHED SCOPE: a 2023–2025 peer-reviewed comparison of task success with native vs ARIA-custom controls among screen-reader users (Q7/Q20 returned grey literature and the two above). Recorded as a gap.
KNOWLEDGE: the rebuild's stated goal of "semantic HTML per surface" is the direction the empirical record supports (S04, A18-RQ3); custom listbox/combobox/menu/slider patterns must be justified per component and keyboard-tested, because `role="menu"` is the single most-broken ARIA pattern measured at web scale.

## Angle (f) — Prompt-based creative interfaces, AI-music co-creation, command palettes

### Five-part record A04 — Choi et al., CHI 2025 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** prompt-based music GenAI "faces limitations in conveying artistic intent with language alone"; how do creators of different expertise actually use it, and what should the interaction be?
2. **Method:** N=17 (6 experts ≥10 y composing, 6 novices <3 y, 5 nonprofessionals), Korean/English bilingual, remote; three commercial tools counterbalanced on separate days — Suno (prompt), Boomy (preset), AWS DeepComposer (motif); 40-min tutorial, ~51 min per tool (M=51.28, SD=10.46), think-aloud (43 h video), prompt logs, 7-point Likert questionnaire (creative expression, uniqueness, NASA-TLX effort ×2, learning, completeness, engagement; comprehensibility ×2, controllability, ownership ×2, collaboration), 70-min interviews, thematic analysis (89 codes); output evaluation by 3 critics + 14 listeners blind to tool/expertise. ~300 min per participant.
3. **Results:** the authors state that with n=6/6/5 "statistical significance was not observed"; Figures 2–4 are descriptive. Preference ranking: 5 of 6 experts, 4 of 6 novices and all nonprofessionals ranked prompt first; motif last for novices and nonprofessionals. **Novices reported the lowest satisfaction and attitude scores for prompting** because they "found it difficult to verbalize their ideas during the modification process"; 10 of 12 composers asked for preset/motif supplements; all experts said temporal edits cannot be expressed in text ("make the crescendo stronger at the 1:30 mark"); 15 of 17 said layout/colour/buttons did not affect preference; critics and listeners rated prompt-made outputs highest, with nonprofessionals' prompt outputs scoring higher than experts'.
4. **Stated limitations:** small groups, uneven genres, three specific commercial tools (Suno's text box "may oversimplify"), 300-min sessions. Analyst: descriptive statistics only; Korean participants; tools as of 2024.
5. **Application here:** the prompt field must not be the only control surface: (i) a conversational refine loop, (ii) multimodal/structured inputs (meta-tags, instrument/parameter chips — the engine's 103 parameters are exactly this layer), (iii) a timeline/temporal editor for experts, and (iv) everyday-language entry for nonprofessionals. The finding that intermediate users are the least served by pure prompting argues for progressive disclosure keyed to expertise, not one field for all.

### Five-part record A05 — "It's more of a vibe I'm going for", DIS 2025 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** video creators describe music as "vibes", cannot articulate musical attributes, and current text-to-music UIs are an open text box.
2. **Method:** formative study → five design goals (rough-cut first; centre on vibe/emotion; use video context; validate against video; structured exploration of multiple options); prototype: LLM recommends an editable vibe from transcript+scene captions, LLM expands it into **three structured prompts (genre, instruments, mood, theme, energy)**, three tracks generated with different seeds; refinement panel splits the prompt into independently editable fields; same seed kept on minor edits. Within-subject probe vs baseline text box, N=7 internal creators, 15-min tasks, 20-item 7-point survey (CSI/Amershi-derived), think-aloud.
3. **Results:** all 7 found vibe recommendations useful; **4/7 preferred the probe**, 2 valued both (baseline when a specific idea existed), 1 (most musical) critical of both; participants "disagree on which system provides more control" — structured fields = clearer for some (P2,P3,P4,P7), "overly constraining" for others (P1,P6); most-requested feature: progressive disclosure / "open mode and guided mode"; survey visualisations exploratory only.
4. **Stated limitations:** N=7, internal employees, one model, no comparison with existing search workflow, song-level control only, seed reuse does not guarantee similarity. Analyst: 15-min tasks; video-editing context, not a listening product.
5. **Application here:** an evidence-backed pattern for the composer surface: a free-text entry that the system reflects back as a structured, editable intermediate representation (our understood tokens/parameters), multiple takes side by side, and an explicit toggle between guided and open prompting. Also a warning: the structured layer must never lock the user out of raw text.

### Prompt-writing support (A25 [ABS], InspirationGraph [ABS], ACAI [ABS], PromptCanvas [ABS])
DesignWeaver (CHI'25, N=52 novices): surfacing design dimensions as a selectable palette produced longer, more domain-specific prompts and more diverse outputs, but "heightened participants' expectations beyond what current models could deliver". InspirationGraph (CHI'26, N=24): dimension–attribute dictionary + editable tree reduced cognitive load. PromptCanvas (lab + 2-week field): persistent widgets externalising prompt structure increased perceived control vs conversational UI. ACAI: panel-based structured inputs for novices. Convergent direction (4 families, all ABS): **structured, persistent, editable prompt scaffolds help novices**; the DIS'25 split shows they must remain optional.

### Command palettes / shortcuts / inline entity recognition
- Remington, Yuen & Pashler 2015 (JEP:Applied, ABS): GUI-vs-shortcut crossover after ≈200 responses on a 20-item list; after training all but one subject kept using shortcuts; adoption tracks retrieval ease. `[single-source]`.
- INTERACT'97 (ABS): recommends a SMALL, generic shortcut set; all-inclusive sets waste effort.
- Command-palette efficiency, slash commands, inline entity/chip recognition in prompt fields: **NOT FOUND IN THE SEARCHED SCOPE** as peer-reviewed work (Q10). Grey sources (HN thread, Vendr history) describe the pattern's three parts — one invocation shortcut, fuzzy matcher, shortcut hints — and its niche (commands used often enough to remember they exist, not often enough to memorise a chord). The Zong CHI'24 command box (BLV users) is the nearest academic datum: quick keystroke commands helped, but helped experienced users most.

## Angle (g) — 3D/immersive media UIs and audio-reactive visualisation

### Five-part record A20 — Fink et al., Scientific Reports 2024 [FULL]
1. **Problem:** does audiovisual correspondence between music and visual art change aesthetic experience? Prior results conflicted and operationalised "correspondence" loosely.
2. **Method:** pre-registered (OSF hjgc5) online experiment, N=201 (207 recruited, 23 countries), 16 pieces from a curated exhibition with music composed for the art; 4 within-subject conditions: Audio, Visual, AV-Intended, AV-Random; self-paced viewing (time spent as implicit interest), 0–100 sliders for mood match, moved, enjoyment, interest, and (AV only) perceived correspondence; RM-ANOVAs with Holm-corrected contrasts; z-scored ratings.
3. **Results:** time spent A 37.08 s > AV-R 32.64 ≈ AV-I 31.68 > V 16.73; F(1.95,390.22)=102.44, p<.001; AV-I vs AV-R n.s. (p=.279). **Correspondence rated higher for intended pairs, t(400)=5.27, p<.001, yet no difference on enjoyment, interest, mood or moved.** Enjoyment F(2.76,552.98)=13.83, p<.001: AV > A; V > A. Interest F=13.06, p<.001: anything with visuals > audio. Participants felt most moved in bimodal conditions; 62 % preferred AV, 15 % V, 12 % A; overall enjoyment 85/100.
4. **Stated limitations:** online, uncontrolled viewing conditions; time-spent proved a poor aesthetic index; art-music context. Analyst: static art, not motion visuals; short pieces.
5. **Application here:** for THE WORLD and the visualiser: adding visuals to audio raises "moved/enjoyment" over audio alone, but *intended* correspondence only registers as perceived correspondence — it does not by itself raise enjoyment. INFERENCE: the visual must be clearly audio-coupled to be *noticed*, and its aesthetic payoff comes from the bimodal experience, not from correspondence fidelity; do not over-invest in beat-exact mapping at the cost of comfort (see angle d).

### Supporting [ABS] evidence
- A21 Frontiers VR 2025: MIR-driven MR visualisation vs randomised same-elements baseline → "slightly enhanced aesthetic experience" (AESTHEMOS); personal musicality did not moderate.
- A19 Cognition 2022: cuts aligned to musical accents (subliminal) → higher perceptual pleasure than misaligned.
- A22 ACM 2025 (hearing + DHH participants): AI audio-responsive visuals correlated moderately with music affect; only arousal correlation significant.
- A35 J. Interactive Systems (N=41, WebXR/React 360 tours on laptop, phone, Cardboard, Gear VR): Cardboard worst overall; performance similar across devices; **unexpected cybersickness on the smartphone**; low usability hypothesised to lower presence.
- S10 Meta Horizon comfort guidance (vendor): teleport/snap over continuous motion, predictable camera, visible avatar signalling upcoming motion, passthrough/stable periphery, avoid sudden acceleration.
Wayfinding-specific WebXR usability studies: NOT FOUND IN THE SEARCHED SCOPE beyond A35 (Q11).
