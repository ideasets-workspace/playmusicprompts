# Outcome first

**Floors met and exceeded:** 19 independent sources consulted; **11 academic**; **8 academic primaries read `[FULL]`** with complete five-part records (slice floor was ≥6 sources / ≥5 academic `[FULL]`). Every load-bearing claim below carries ≥3 independent sources or a visible `[single-source]` flag.

**The seven design-bearing findings, each with its strongest number:**

1. **Genre chips are the single highest-yield control.** In real prompts, genre is near-ubiquitous: **~95% of Udio prompts contain genre language and genre occupies 38.8% of prompt vocabulary** (A-03), and genre/style is the most-used tag class on Suno and Udio (P(≥1 genre tag) = .68 Suno / .98 Udio, A-02). Genre words are also the ONLY prompt words (with instruments) that reliably survive from prompt → audio → listener perception (A-03, Table 2).
2. **Mood words are what users FEEL but do not TYPE — a mood picker adds information the prompt field will not capture on its own.** Mood/Emotion appears in prompts far below its rate in listening descriptions (presence interaction β = −3.0 to −3.7, p<.001, A-03), yet prompts that DO specify mood are 3.3× more common in the high semantic-alignment group (52% vs 16%, A-03 Table 3). Chips should therefore actively OFFER mood terms rather than expect them.
3. **Story/scene prompts are the top misalignment risk.** Narrative language dominates low-alignment prompts (density 0.452 vs 0.169, A-03) — "new york cafe"-class scene stations must be compiled server-side into acoustic terms (genre/instrument/mood/tempo), never passed raw to the model.
4. **The real preference space behind the chips is 5-factor, not N-genre.** The MUSIC model (Mellow, Unpretentious, Sophisticated, Intense, Contemporary) replicates across 3 studies/2 stimulus sets/26 subgenres (A-01) and across 53 countries (S-10); attributes (relaxing, loud, fast, instrumental…) predict factor placement at multiple R .67–.83 before genre is even added (A-01 Table 5). Chip rows should cover all five factors, and energy/valence sliders are evidence-grounded controls.
5. **For music-specific mood vocabulary, 9 GEMS terms suffice for a picker.** GEMS-9 (one term per dimension) reproduces the full 45-item instrument's emotion profiles at ICC = .89 with maximum-value scoring (A-07); music-evoked emotion is domain-specific — generic emotion checklists fit worse (A-06 `[ABS]`, corroborated A-07, S-9).
6. **Structured controls measurably beat a bare prompt box for novices — but must not replace it.** Cococo's steering controls raised novices' creative-goal expression from 3.8→5.5/7 (p=.0006) and self-efficacy 3.7→5.9 (p<.0001) (A-05); non-experts prompt opportunistically, over-generalise from single failures, and avoid known-effective phrasing (A-04); novices' preferred abstraction level is heterogeneous (S-13/S-14) — so ship prompt field + chips TOGETHER, chips composing INTO the prompt.
7. **The instrumental option has hard cognitive evidence for work/study stations.** Lyrics impair verbal memory, visual memory and reading (d ≈ −0.3) while instrumental lo-fi shows no credible harm (A-08); replicated by 4 self-paced-reading experiments (S-11) and a Bayesian meta-analysis (g = −0.19 reading, S-12 via A-08). Default "office hours"-class stations to instrumental; keep lyrics/instrumental/random as a first-class 3-state control (11–15.6% of ALL Suno/Udio generations are instrumental, and voice tags reach P(≥1)=.46 on Udio — A-02).

**Committed recommendation for the page (this slice's synthesis):** one free prompt field (always visible, never gated) + one genre chip row spanning all 5 MUSIC factors + one 9-term GEMS-anchored mood row (each term carrying a valence–arousal coordinate for the engine) + scene/era stations compiled to acoustic descriptors + a 3-state vocal control (default instrumental for focus scenes) + one visible "surprise me" + regenerate-with-variations. Every chip writes visibly into the prompt text (casual-creator "no blank canvas" + Cococo transparency evidence).

# Five-part depth records (R9) — the eight `[FULL]` academic primaries

## A-01 — Rentfrow, Goldberg & Levitin 2011, JPSP 100(6):1139–1157 `[FULL]`

1. **Problem (authors' framing).** "Individuals demonstrate manifestly different preferences in music, and yet relatively little is known about the underlying structure of those preferences." Past work relied on genre self-report, but "genres are extremely broad and ill-defined categories, so measurements based solely on genres are necessarily crude and imprecise" — genre labels also activate social stereotypes, confounding what is being preferred.
2. **Method.** Four studies. Study 1: 706 internet respondents rated liking (1–9) of 52 fifteen-second excerpts covering 26 genres/subgenres selected via a 5,600-person pilot + 10 industry experts; PCA/PA/ML factor extractions compared (cross-method factor-score correlations >.99); hierarchical structure via Goldberg's top-down method; 5-month follow-up with a 64-excerpt vocal/instrumental-balanced revision (n=75). Study 2: replication with 94 NEVER-released Getty excerpts, N=354. Study 3: in-person replication with 25 excerpts, N=817 students (half rated excerpts, half rated the genre of each excerpt; factor congruence .97). Study 4: 40 untrained judges rated all 146 excerpts on 7 sound attributes + 7 psychological attributes; hierarchical regressions of factor loadings on attributes vs genres.
3. **Real numbers.** Five factors — Mellow, Unpretentious, Sophisticated, Intense, Contemporary (MUSIC). First unrotated factor = 27% variance (Study 1), 26% (Study 2). Factor structure invariant to sex/age (congruence .99–.999). Attribute agreement: instrumental α=.99, aggressive α=.93. Table 4 exemplars: Intense ↔ distorted r=.67, loud .64, aggressive .66, relaxing −.54; Mellow ↔ romantic .57, relaxing .56, fast −.43; Sophisticated ↔ electric −.66, intelligent .58, inspiring .55; Contemporary ↔ rap .51, percussive... (electric .32); Unpretentious ↔ new country .46, aggressive −.48. Table 5: attributes alone predict factor placement at multiple R = .67 (Mellow) to .83 (Intense); attributes add significant variance OVER genres for 4/5 factors (F = 2.58–4.64, p<.05).
4. **Stated limitations.** Internet/self-selected samples; possible excerpt-vs-genre rating ambiguity; attributes examined were mostly timbre-related — pitch/rhythm/melodic attributes not coded; generational/cultural generality untested (the factors may persist while their genre contents shift over time).
5. **Application here.** (a) The genre chip row must span all five factors, not just popular genres — a row of only Contemporary+Intense genres leaves Mellow/Sophisticated/Unpretentious listeners without an entry point. (b) Because preference is substantially attribute-driven (R .67–.83 before genre), attribute controls (energy/relaxing↔intense, acoustic↔electric, instrumental) are legitimate first-class inputs, mapping directly onto the engine's 35-parameter contract. (c) The authors' own recommendation-system note — MUSIC factors "may capture the latent structure of individual music preferences better than traditional genre labels" — supports scoring skip-adaptive taste in 5-factor space rather than per-genre.

## A-02 — Casini et al. 2026, TISMIR 9(1) doi:10.5334/tismir.273 `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** "There has been no systematic analysis of how these [text-to-music] systems are actually being used in practice" — what languages, what characterises prompts/lyrics/tags, how artist names and metatags are used.
2. **Method.** 101,953 songs by 60,342 users scraped from Suno (81,434) and Udio (20,519) "new songs" playlists, May–Oct 2024. NV-Embed-v2 (4096-d) embeddings → UMAP (5-d) → HDBSCAN clustering; fastText language ID; manually refined high-level tag taxonomy (genre/instrument via Every Noise at Once + IMIT instrument list; regex for BPM/year/key); Poisson fits per tag class per platform.
3. **Real numbers.** 41,108 songs have prompts; median prompt ≈ **80 characters**; 16,881 English deduped prompts → **81 clusters**, mostly genre- or instrument-anchored, plus theme clusters (pets, coffee, summer). Two prompt styles: comma-separated qualifier lists vs literary descriptions ("A song about…") — a spectrum, most prompts mixing both. Tags: 24,058 unique, 76% appear once; tags used >10 times = 1,245 across 8 classes. Per-generation expected tag counts (Suno/Udio): GENRE/STYLE E=1.15/3.90, P(≥1)=.68/.98; QUALIFIER/MOOD P(≥1)=.57/.97; INSTRUMENT P(≥1)=.22/.23; VOICE P(≥1)=.12/.46 (male/female voice form two compact clusters); YEAR/DECADE P(≥1)=.029/.053; KEY P(≥1)≈.004; BPM P(≥1)≈.002. Instrumental songs: **15.6% (Suno) / 11.1% (Udio)**. Languages (lyrics): English 46.75%/71.39%, German 8.87%/3.68%, Russian 6.68%/2.99%, Turkish **1.66%/0.29%**. Artist references: 1,000 replacement events, 703 unique artists in 20,519 Udio songs (top: XXXTentacion 26, Drake 19, Taylor Swift 18); Udio REPLACES artist names with descriptor bundles ("male vocalist, synthpop, downtempo…"), Suno blocks them. Metatags in lyrics: [verse] 88,270, [chorus] 65,342, [bridge] 22,491, [guitar solo] 2,047.
4. **Stated limitations.** Six-month window; published-songs-only bias; English-focused analyses; platform version differences uncaptured; HDBSCAN leaves 25,794 lyric outliers; scraping was against ToS (justified under EU 2019/790 research exception).
5. **Application here.** (a) Control priority order justified by measured frequency: genre → qualifier/mood → instrument → voice → decade; key/BPM pickers are NOT casual-tier controls (P(≥1)≤.005). (b) The prompt placeholder should model the dominant real pattern: comma-separated genre+qualifier list around ~80 chars. (c) A voice/vocal-gender option is used when offered (Udio .46 vs Suno .12 — interface prominence drives usage), supporting the vocal control and an optional voice-type refinement. (d) Artist-name inputs are real user demand (703 artists) but must be compiled into descriptor bundles exactly as Udio does — provider-terms-safe. (e) 11–15.6% instrumental share = the instrumental toggle is a mainstream need, not an edge case.

## A-03 — "From Prompting to Describing" 2026, arXiv 2608.06634 `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** "It is unclear whether the descriptive language used to prompt aligns with descriptive language used to summarize or describe heard music" — prompting encodes anticipatory intent; describing is grounded perception; TTM models train on metadata-like text, not natural listening language.
2. **Method.** 200 real Udio prompts (sampled from A-02's corpus) regenerated to 32-s clips; free-form descriptions from n=70 English + n=78 Korean listeners (2,624 descriptions); human-derived 7-category prompt taxonomy (Genre, Mood/Emotion, Instrumentation, Music Theory, Timbre, Function, Story/Narrative) built by dual annotators; GPT-based category coding validated on a human-annotated held-out set; mixed-effects GLMM/LMM (lme4) with text/participant/stimulus random intercepts; word survival rates; χ² prompt→description associations; Sentence-BERT cosine alignment; Brysbaert concreteness norms.
3. **Real numbers.** Prompts: Genre presence ≈ **.95**, Story/Narrative ≈ .74; genre density **38.8%** of prompt words vs 15.2% in descriptions. Mood/Emotion, Instrumentation, Music Theory, Timbre, Function all appear LESS in prompts than descriptions (presence interactions β −3.00 to −3.72, p<.001). Descriptions: Instrumentation is the only category more present than genre (β=0.28, p<.001; density 21.1%). Word survival: country 73.8%, guitar 37.6%, rock 36.2%; no mood or story word met the association threshold; listeners substitute neighbours (metal→rock/punk; country→banjo). High- vs low-alignment prompt quartiles: Mood 52% vs 16%, Instrumentation 70% vs 18%, Music Theory 48% vs 26%, Timbre 38% vs 6%; Story/Narrative density 0.169 vs **0.452** (≈3× in the misaligned group). Cross-cultural: Korean listeners allocate more density to Mood (16.6% vs 13.1%, p<.001), Story (15.3% vs 13.5%) and Function (10.4% vs 8.9%); English more to Genre (15.2% vs 11.8%) and Music Theory.
4. **Stated limitations.** Single system (Udio); recruitment channels differ between language groups (cross-cultural findings exploratory); LLM-coded categories introduce classification noise.
5. **Application here.** (a) Genre chips carry the most reliably-transmitted signal — they deserve the top row. (b) Mood, instrument and timbre terms are exactly what casual users under-supply yet what most improves prompt–output alignment (Δ presence +.36/+.52/+.22 in the aligned quartile): chips must OFFER these categories. (c) Scene/story stations ("new york cafe") match how users naturally speak (74% of prompts carry narrative) but are the top misalignment source — the Intent-Vector compiler must translate scenes into acoustic descriptors server-side. (d) Non-English users skew even more affective/functional: TR-locale placeholder examples should lead with mood+function, not genre jargon.

## A-04 — Zamfirescu-Pereira, Wong, Hartmann & Yang, CHI 2023 `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** "Surprisingly little is known about how non-experts intuitively approach designing prompts… and how effective they are in doing so." Prompting looks effortless but effective strategies require systematic error identification and evaluation.
2. **Method.** Design probe: BotDesigner, a no-code chatbot design tool (prompt template + conversation view + error browser with labeling and regression-style re-testing) on GPT-3 text-davinci-002. 10 participants without prompt-design experience improved a recipe-instruction bot toward a target human performance (Back-to-Back Chef); think-aloud; affinity-diagram analysis; struggles mapped to end-user-programming barrier taxonomy.
3. **Real numbers.** N=10 (professions from psychology professor to product manager; programming experience none→professional). 2/10 needed help to start iterating at all. **0/10 used systematic testing**; participants declared success from a SINGLE good output and abandoned strategies after a SINGLE failure ("ok, I guess it doesn't like to tell jokes"). Only 1/10 independently added example dialogue, though examples were observed to be highly effective; 2 participants called examples "cheating." Everyone at some point asked "why did it do that?"; do-not-X instructions frequently backfired vs do-Y phrasing.
4. **Stated limitations.** Small, STEM-skewed early-adopter sample; 1-hour sessions; chatbot domain may amplify social-expectation effects; no prevalence claims.
5. **Application here.** This is the "prompt anxiety" evidence base: casual users over-generalise from one bad generation and quit. For the player page: (a) chips/templates function as the "searchable repository of working examples" the authors call for — each chip is a known-good prompt fragment, removing the design/selection barriers; (b) after a disliked track, the UI should offer one-tap variation (skip-adaptive) rather than relying on the user to re-phrase, because users won't systematically iterate; (c) placeholder text should SHOW a working example prompt (show-don't-tell inversion: users won't produce examples, so the product must).

## A-05 — Louie, Coenen, Huang, Terry & Cai, CHI 2020 (Cococo) `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** Generative music AI "can overwhelm users with the amount of musical content it generates, and frustrate them with its non-deterministic output" — novices experience information overload and cannot express goals, so what interactive controls do they actually need?
2. **Method.** Needfinding interviews (n=11 music novices) → design of 4 AI-steering tools on Coconet (Voice Lanes; Example-based similarity slider; Semantic sliders happy/sad = most-likely major/minor triad soft prior, conventional/surprising = sampling temperature on log scale [1/8,2]; Multiple Alternatives, default 3) via a soft-priors technique p_adjusted ∝ p_model × p_softprior (no retraining). Summative within-subjects study N=21 novices, counterbalanced vs a conventional infill interface; 15-min image-inspired composition tasks; paired t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg (Q=.05); thematic analysis.
3. **Real numbers.** Cococo vs conventional (7-pt scales): creative expression **5.5 vs 3.8** (p=.0006); self-efficacy **5.9 vs 3.7** (p<.0001); learning 4.9 vs 3.8 (p=.0003); engagement 6.0 vs 4.4 (p=.0001); controllability **5.9 vs 3.5** (p<.0001); comprehensibility 5.3 vs 3.2 (p<.0001); collaboration 5.9 vs 4.0; trust-capability 6.1 vs 4.8; ownership **5.2 vs 3.8** (p=.0071); attribution-to-self 4.6 vs 3.4 (p=.0136); completeness 5.0 vs 3.7 (p=.0116). Effort n.s. (4.1 vs 4.8). Uniqueness n.s. 12/21 adjusted sliders before their FIRST generation.
4. **Stated limitations.** Perception measures, not expert-judged output quality; cannot fully separate soft-prior effect from merely having more controls; novices sometimes blamed themselves for AI failures; incomplete mental models discouraged experimentation.
5. **Application here.** The strongest direct evidence that a small set of semantic controls (happy/sad ↔ valence; conventional/surprising ↔ a "surprise" control; alternatives to audition) measurably raises casual users' expression, ownership and trust IN MUSIC specifically. For the page: (a) mood/energy chips and a surprise control are not decoration — they carried p<.001 effects; (b) generate ≥2–3 alternatives per prompt (the hidden 3-track buffer already matches "Multiple Alternatives"); (c) controls must be optional — ownership came partly from choice availability ("There are options, but I don't feel like I have to use them"); (d) expose what a control maps to (their onboarding recommendation) — e.g. label the surprise control honestly.

## A-07 — Jacobsen, Strauss, Vigl, Zangerle & Zentner 2024, Musicae Scientiae `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** The GEMS-9 one-item-per-dimension checklist is increasingly used in place of the 45-item GEMS, but "it remains at present unclear whether the two instruments produce comparable scores."
2. **Method.** 192 participants (M age 27.5; 105 German-, 87 English-speaking; Innsbruck pool + Prolific) rated 18 excerpts (6 each hip-hop/rap, pop, classical; mean 46 s; drawn from the EMMA database with ICC>.75) with BOTH instruments in two sessions (scan-select procedure; 0–100 visual-analog scales). GEMS-45 scored two ways: maximum-value (highest item = scale score) and weighted-mean. Concordance via ICC(A,1) absolute agreement; interrater via ICC(C,k).
3. **Real numbers.** Interrater agreement ICC(C,k) = **.95–.99** for both instruments. GEMS-45↔GEMS-9 profile concordance: weighted-mean scoring mean ICC = .67 (range .28–.85); **maximum-value scoring mean ICC = .89** (SD=.06, range .68–.94). Largest discrepancy on Wonder (its GEMS-45 scale contains "happy"; the single-item version does not). Structure restated: 9 dimensions (wonder, transcendence, tenderness, nostalgia, peacefulness, joyful activation, power, tension, sadness) under 3 superfactors (Sublimity, Vitality, Unease).
4. **Stated limitations.** 18 excerpts from 3 genres — generalisation to other music open; scan-select (not all-item) instruction; induced (felt) not perceived emotion; GEMS-9 cannot replace in-depth assessment.
5. **Application here.** A 9-term music-specific mood vocabulary is measurement-grade: single-word chips named after the GEMS dimensions reproduce full-instrument emotion profiles at ICC .89. For the page: the mood row can be exactly 9 chips (casual tier), each mapping to engine mood/valence-arousal parameters; the 3 superfactors give the grouping if the row needs visual clustering; excerpt-level emotion profiles stabilise at ~10–20 raters — relevant later for crowd-tuning station presets.

## A-08 — Souza & Barbosa 2023, Journal of Cognition 6(1):24 `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** "People often listen to music while doing cognitive tasks. Yet, whether music harms or helps performance is still debated" — and the lyrics-vs-instrumental moderator plus users' own metacognition are under-measured.
2. **Method.** Within-subjects, N=113–123 (Porto psychology students; power 80% for d=.26). Four tasks (verbal recall 20 words; visual colour recall; reading comprehension 2×20 sentences; arithmetic 2×20 problems) × three conditions (silence; instrumental **lo-fi hip-hop** from Lofi Girl; Portuguese chart pop WITH lyrics). Bayesian inference (BayesFactor defaults); judgments of learning during task + post-task ratings.
3. **Real numbers.** Lyrics vs silence: verbal recall d = **−.32** (BF₁₀=20.4); visual recall d = **−.36** (BF₁₀=74.4); reading accuracy d = −.33 (BF₁₀=40.8), reading also slower; arithmetic d = −.19 (BF₁₀=0.53, not credible). Instrumental vs silence: d = −.16 to +.14, none credible; instrumental beat lyrical directly on reading (d=.29, BF₁₀=9.66). Reading was the ONE task where instrumental trended positive. Participants were AWARE lyrics hurt (post-task ratings <0) yet retrospectively rated instrumental as beneficial. 57% study with music at least sometimes.
4. **Stated limitations.** Online administration (ambient noise uncontrolled); lyric/instrumental songs differed in more than lyric presence; sample under-powered for very small instrumental effects; college-student, mostly female sample.
5. **Application here.** Direct evidence for the vocal control's DEFAULTS: focus/work/study stations ("office hours", study lo-fi) should default to instrumental — lyrics measurably cost d≈0.3 on exactly the tasks those scenes accompany, while instrumental lo-fi (the same genre our users will generate) is safe. It also explains user behaviour: people know lyrics distract, so an easy instrumental switch matches existing metacognition.

## A-09 — Compton & Mateas 2015, ICCC (Casual Creators) `[FULL]`

1. **Problem.** Autotelic creativity tools — where the pleasure of creating is the point, not the product — "have been overlooked in academic research"; there is no shared design language for them.
2. **Method.** Definitional/design-pattern paper grounded in theory (flow, reflection-in-action, direct manipulation, creativity-support principles) plus case studies built with the patterns (PendantMaker, IceMaker, Funky Ikebana, BotPrint, Binary Fission). Definition: "encourages the fast, confident, and pleasurable exploration of a possibility space, resulting in the creation or discovery of surprising new artifacts that bring feelings of pride, ownership, and creativity."
3. **Real results (qualitative patterns; no empirical tables — stated per R9.3).** Patterns: **instant feedback**; **no blank canvas** ("the first move is the hardest, so this restricts the first move to a single decision: accept the prompt, or discard it"); **limiting actions to encourage exploration** (choices should be "appropriate, limited in number", modal); **mutant shopping** (browse generated alternatives; picking 1 of 10 avoided choice paralysis in Funky Ikebana); **modifying the meaningful** (high-level handles beat low-level parameters); **entertaining evaluations**; **saving and sharing**. Anti-pattern: "1000 bowls of oatmeal" (technically distinct, perceptually uniform outputs). Casual users trade control for "the speed, power, and surprise of generative methods."
4. **Stated limitations.** Pattern catalogue is "not exhaustive"; case studies are the authors' own systems; no controlled evaluation in this paper (controlled evidence arrives later via A-05, which explicitly builds on this frame).
5. **Application here.** The player IS a casual creator; the patterns are the page's grammar: pre-filled/suggested prompt (no blank canvas — one decision: accept or edit); a bounded chip set rather than exhaustive menus (limiting actions); "surprise me" + regenerate = mutant shopping over the generation space; chips as high-level handles (modifying the meaningful, e.g. "dreamy" not "reverb wet 0.4"); make-playlist/share = pride & ownership loop; and station presets must be tuned against the oatmeal anti-pattern (distinct stations must SOUND distinct).
