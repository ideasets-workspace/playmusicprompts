# Outcome first

1. **The interaction frontier for prompt-to-music is "vibe in, structure out, then levels of control".** Two independent 2025 HCI studies (CHI '25 Choi et al., 17 participants; DIS '25 Fraser et al., 11 + 7 participants) converge on the same finding from different populations and methods: natural-language prompting is the preferred ENTRY for non-experts, but users cannot express TEMPORAL and structural intent in language, and the fix is (a) an editable intermediate structured representation (genre / instruments / mood / theme / energy boxes; meta tags), (b) multiple takes generated at once for comparison (three in both studies), and (c) the ability to move between a "guided" and an "open" mode. PlayMusicPrompts already has the data for this: `prompt_sent` and `bindings.<param>.sent` ARE the intermediate representation, and "Make it a playlist = 3 takes" IS the multi-option pattern. [Claim C1, 3 sources]
2. **Transparency of the machine's state is the trust lever for generative playback.** NIME 2026 Zhu & Martin (n=5, SUS 62.5) and the IASDR Talaria curation study (14 professional curators) both find that exposing WHAT the model did (provenance, per-parameter interpretation, "human vs machine" colour-coded contributions) raises trust and agency even when output quality is imperfect. The API's `bindings`, `route.why`, `mix_plan.refusals[]` and `render_plan.stages.master.verification` are exactly this evidence and are currently hidden from the listener. [C2, 3 sources]
3. **Latency is a design material, not an engineering footnote.** Roads' time scales (micro 1–100 ms; meso 1–2 s; macro) frame the whole generative-player problem: a text-prompted model answers at macro/meso scale (Stable Audio Open Small 187 ms on GPU; Lyria RealTime "control latency maximum 2 seconds"), so the interface must PRE-GENERATE and roll back. NIME 2026 Smith & Pardo measured that speculative execution brings perceived latency from +213 ms to −26 ms (Top-15, 2.6 % error) or 77 ms (Top-16, 0 % error). The owner's buffer invariant (≥2 prepared tracks, generation at track start, recalibration at T−90 s) is a macro-scale speculative-execution scheme; the UI should SHOW the prepared takes as the visible manifestation of that buffer. [C3, 3 sources]
4. **Beat/downbeat-driven visuals should use a transformer beat tracker without DBN post-processing, but the continuity trade-off must be designed around.** Beat This! (ISMIR 2024, Foscarin/Schlüter/Widmer, CPJKU) reaches GTZAN beat F1 89.1 ± 0.3 / downbeat F1 78.3 ± 0.4 with ~20 M params and a ~2 M-param variant at 88.8 / 77.2, but CMLt/AMLt fall (79.8 / 89.8 vs Hung 81.2 / 92.0) because the network emits non-periodic beats in hard passages. For a visualiser this means: use the ±70 ms peak-pick output for hit events, but smooth the DISPLAYED tempo with a phase-locked loop or the DBN variant (which recovers downbeat CMLt 73.3) so the visual does not stutter. [C4, 3 sources]
5. **Dark-vs-light: the peer-reviewed record favours positive polarity for READING and cognitive tasks, including in low light; no controlled study found favours dark for a music-player transport task.** Ergonomics 2025 (N=173): light M=4.92 vs dark M=4.55, F(1,172)=9.61, p=.002, ηp²=.05. Piepenbrock et al. 2014 (pupil size mechanism). Bentley thesis review: Buchner & Baumgartner 2007 and Dobres et al. 2016 found positive-polarity advantage persists at 5 lux / dim light. A 2026 within-subjects experiment (N=80, Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.20687423, `[ABS]`) reports light faster (d=+0.52) but dark lower eye strain (d=−0.29) and recommends user-controlled theme. **This is evidence, not a verdict: the owner decides** (CONTEXT-18). [C5, 3 sources; the tension with the black+neon mockups is preserved, not resolved]
6. **Accessibility floor for the transport is codified and checkable:** WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 (AA) 24×24 CSS px targets or 24 px spacing circles; SC 2.5.7 (AA) seek/volume must not be drag-only; SC 2.4.11 focus not obscured; SC 1.4.11 3:1 non-text contrast for control chrome — the last is the one that bites neon-on-black. Reduced motion: WCAG 2.3.3 (AAA) and 2.2.2 (A) via `prefers-reduced-motion`, which does NOT govern JS/GPU animation loops, so the visualiser needs its own `matchMedia` gate. [C6, 3 sources]
7. **Motion language frontier is physics (springs), split into spatial vs effects.** Material 3 Expressive (May 2025) replaced duration/easing with spring tokens (stiffness, damping, initial velocity), two schemes (expressive/standard), spatial springs allowed to overshoot, effects (opacity/colour) near-critically damped "because bouncing alpha reads as a glitch". Google states 46 studies / 18,000+ participants behind it — `[single-source official]`, the studies themselves are not published. [C7]
8. **Real-time generative steering already has a shipped public API shape:** Lyria RealTime (Gemini API, `lyria-realtime-exp`, updated May 2025): WebSocket bidi stream, 48 kHz stereo PCM, `WeightedPrompt` list, config `bpm [60,200]`, `density [0,1]`, `brightness [0,1]`, `guidance [0,6]`, `scale`, `muteBass/muteDrums/onlyBassAndDrums`, `musicGenerationMode QUALITY|DIVERSITY|VOCALIZATION`; PromptDJ / PromptDJ-MIDI demos (sliders per prompt weight, WebMIDI knobs). This is the reference interaction model for "the music changes while it plays". Note the API angle belongs to another worker; only the HCI shape is recorded here. [C8, `[single-source official]` for exact ranges]

# Framing and falsifiers

- **Hypothesis H1:** prompt + structured fields + multi-take comparison is the frontier entry pattern. *Falsifier:* a 2025–2026 controlled study showing pure free-text prompting outperforms structured refinement for non-experts on control/satisfaction. Not found in the searched scope; the DIS '25 study shows a SPLIT (experts prefer direct prompting), which is recorded as a contradiction, not averaged.
- **H2:** exposing model state increases trust. *Falsifier:* a study where provenance display reduced enjoyment or increased load without trust gain. Zhu & Martin's SUS 62.5 (below the 68 average) shows a real learnability COST alongside the trust gain — recorded.
- **H3:** positive polarity aids reading even in low light. *Falsifier:* Rempel 2012 (cited in the Bentley thesis) reports a response-time advantage for negative polarity in the dark on an identification task — recorded as the one contrary datum.
- **H4:** DBN-free beat tracking is SOTA on F1. *Falsifier:* the 2026 arXiv 2605.12287 "SMC blind spot" analysis (`[ABS]`) argues a learned adaptive DBN could recover continuity — recorded as open.

### S01 — Choi et al., CHI '25, "Understanding the Potentials and Limitations of Prompt-based Music Generative AI" [FULL]

1. **Problem (authors' framing):** prompt-based music GenAI "faces limitations in conveying artistic intent with language alone", and "the analysis of user experiences with prompt-based music GenAI is still in its early stages" (capture lines 9, 25).
2. **Method:** 18 recruited / 17 analysed participants in three expertise groups (6 expert composers ≥10 yrs, 6 novices, 5 nonprofessionals); three commercial tools mapped to interaction types — Boomy (preset), AWS DeepComposer (motif), SUNO (prompt); remote, counterbalanced order, ~51.28 min per tool (min 45.11, max 58, SD 10.46), think-aloud, 7-point Likert questionnaire (creative expression, uniqueness, NASA-TLX effort ×2, learning, completeness, engagement; comprehensibility ×2, controllability, ownership ×2, collaboration), ~70-min semi-structured interviews; 300 min per participant; ~43 h video, 51 survey files, 120 pages transcripts; thematic analysis, 89 codes; additional blind evaluation of outputs by 3 critics + 14 listeners (lines 67–69, 100–122, 166–170).
3. **Quantitative results:** authors state "Due to the small sample sizes … statistical significance was not observed" — descriptive only (line 120). Figure 2: prompt type ranked first by most experts (n=5) and most novices (n=4); motif least preferred by novices and nonprofessionals. Figure 3: novices reported lower satisfaction/attitude for prompt than the other groups; most participants rated prompt "difficult to control" (Fig 3-h). Figure 4: critics and audience rated prompt-based outputs highest across groups; nonprofessional prompt outputs rated higher than expert/novice prompt outputs. n=15 of 17 said layout/buttons/colours did not affect preference; n=4 found DeepComposer's layout initially overwhelming (line 146). No table of means is printed in the capture — figure-only.
4. **Limitations:** authors — small per-group samples, uneven genre representation, three tools confound interaction type with product (Boomy copyright-free library, DeepComposer free tier, SUNO text-only), 300-min sessions (lines 230–232). Analyst — commercial tools of early 2024–25; Suno version not pinned; Korean-composer sample (K-pop genres named) limits cultural generality.
5. **Application here:** (a) Generate screen (web `/`, Flutter Home/Generate): keep the free-text `prompt` as entry, but expose `bindings.<param>.sent` after generation as an editable structured layer — this is the paper's "multimodal communication" guideline (§6.2) realised with data the API already returns. (b) Player/Studio: the temporal-control finding (§6.3, "make the crescendo stronger at 1:30") maps to `structure` (`[{section,bars}]`), `energy_curve` (envelope control shape) and `duration.target_seconds` — surface them on a timeline, not a form. (c) Differentiate by expertise: Home = nonprofessional path; Studio (all 101 params) = expert path; add an intermediate "step-by-step feedback" path for novices (§6.2 bullet 2).

### S02 — Fraser et al., DIS '25, "It's more of a vibe I'm going for" [FULL]

1. **Problem:** video creators "struggle to describe their musical goals and compare options"; T2M models "often require musical expertise and are difficult to control" (line 23).
2. **Method:** formative study n=11 (60-min remote sessions, task walkthrough, "music supervisor" prompt), inductive coding → five design goals DG1–DG5 (rough-cut first; centre on vibe; consider video context; validate against video; structured exploration of multiple options). Prototype: LLM (GPT-4-Turbo) recommends an editable "vibe" from transcript (Speechmatics) + captions (CLIP+LLaVA); LLM expands vibe into THREE structured prompts (`genre`, `instruments`, `mood`, `theme`, `energy`), each sent with a different random seed to an internal licensed-instrumental T2M model (up to 5 min); three results land on three timeline tracks; refinement edits the structured boxes, re-generate keeps the seed if prompt changed, new seed if unchanged; Previous/Next song navigation. User study n=7 (80-min sessions, within-subjects vs a single-text-box baseline, 20-item 7-point survey adapted from CSI + Amershi et al., 40-min interview); "small sample makes statistical testing inappropriate" — divergent stacked bars and slope graphs (lines 61–182, 184–188).
3. **Quantitative results:** 4/7 preferred the probe, 2/7 both, 1/7 (most musically expert) critical of both; all 7 found vibe recommendations useful (Fig 6); exploration rated stronger for probe (Figs 7–8); clarity higher for probe (Fig 9); feedback split (Fig 10). Formative: 9/11 began by narrating video context; 85 % of TikTok / 84 % of YouTube videos contain music (2023, cited). No effect sizes reported.
4. **Limitations:** authors — n=7, one T2M model, no comparison with existing search workflows, song-level control only, seed reuse does not guarantee similarity (§7). Analyst — participants recruited inside "a large tech company" (Adobe per acks), so novelty bias and employer effect are plausible; video-editing context differs from a listening product.
5. **Application here:** (a) "Make it a playlist = 3 takes" is exactly the paper's three-prompt/three-seed exploration; the UI should show the three takes side by side as first-class objects with their structured prompts visible, not as a hidden buffer. (b) Offer "guided" (vibe-first) and "open" (direct prompt) modes as the paper's participants explicitly requested (§5.5.2, P4/P7). (c) On re-generate keep `seed` when the user edits a field, randomise when they do not — a rule the API supports (`seed` is `typed`, param 34).

### S04 — Smith & Pardo, NIME '26, "Shifting Time Scales … Speculative Execution" [FULL]

1. **Problem:** AI NIMEs "have focused on coarse, text-based instructions delivered at time scales that are not suitable for fine-grained control", and "audio generation introduces latency that impedes gesture-based control" (abstract).
2. **Method:** Roads' time scales (supra/macro/meso 1–2 s/micro 1–100 ms) as evaluation frame. Gesture Vocabulary: MediaPipe hand tracking → hand-sign (2-hidden-layer linear) + motion (LSTM) classifiers → text label → Stable Audio Open Small (DiT). Speculative execution: an online-trained PyTorch LSTM predicts gesture END location on a 4×4 grid (16 outcomes, each mapped to an instrument prompt); audio for Top-N predictions is generated BEFORE the gesture ends; on mismatch the output "rolls back" (game rollback-netcode analogy). Baselines: long spline (3.4 s linear extrapolation) and short spline (220 ms). Metrics: perceived latency = t_audio_start − t_gesture_end (can be negative); error rate = % predictions whose pre-generated audio ≠ actual end cell. 25-minute continuous session, 30-frame segments, 20 fps, prediction horizon 670 frames ≈ 3.6 s (lines 70–96).
3. **Results (Table 3, Fig 5):** baseline no-Spec-Ex 213 ms (MediaPipe 25.4 ms + SAO Small 187 ms), 0 % error by construction. LSTM Top-1: −3.1 s, 66 % error; Top-15: −26 ms, 2.6 % error; Top-16: +77 ms, 0 % error. Long spline −3.2 s / 55 %; short spline −110 ms / 34 %. Top-6 crosses the short spline 2 s before completion at 33 % error. Random 15-of-16 would be 6.25 % error. Hardware: 2022 Apple M2 laptop.
4. **Limitations:** authors — 16 discrete options only, discrete gestures, no speed/trajectory affordances, single-user 25-min evaluation (§6). Analyst — no participants beyond the author; latency numbers are prototype-specific; "perceived latency" excludes audio-buffer output latency.
5. **Application here:** the owner's algorithm (generate at track START; recalibrate at T−90 s; ≥2 prepared takes; skip → next prepared take instantly) IS macro-scale speculative execution. Design consequence: the Player must (a) make the "prepared" state visible (prepared takes as cards/tiles whose readiness is real, fed by `takes_delivered` and `tracks[]`), (b) treat skip as a rollback with an instant crossfade rather than a spinner, (c) budget the visualiser so a skip commits within Roads' micro window (<100 ms visual response) even though audio generation is macro-scale.

### S05 — Zhu & Martin, NIME '26, "A Web Interface for Real-Time Interaction with ML in Musical Performance" [FULL]

1. **Problem:** IML music tools "function as black boxes … limiting the performer's sense of agency and trust" (abstract).
2. **Method:** React frontend + Python 3.11 backend (Keras/TF MDRNN, IMPSY), MIDI for control, WebSocket for UI state, REST for config; Performance View with dual-path visualisation — D3 bar graphs of instantaneous control values (InputVis/OutputVis) and a canvas flow-field particle system (BackgroundVis) where human input = cool tones (blue/teal), AI predictions = warm (red/orange), rendered at the exact moment the corresponding MIDI event is scheduled; Training View shows live loss; Project Dashboard exposes dataset lineage. 3. **Results (Table 2 and §4.2.1):** SUS mean 62.50 (σ 11.9): P1 56.25, P2 64.58, P3 58.33, P4 81.25, P5 52.08; industry average 68 cited. Item "Real-time feedback clarity" mean 4.0/5 (highest); learnability item mean 2.6/5 (friction). Observation: all 5 glanced at the particle canvas before adjusting input.
4. **Limitations:** authors — n=5 exploratory, single 40-min session, sparse training data confounds usability with output quality (§5). Show generation progress as a live, honest state (the API's `render_plan.stages.*.ok`) rather than an indeterminate spinner.

### S27 — Foscarin, Schlüter, Widmer, ISMIR 2024, "Beat This! Accurate beat tracking without DBN postprocessing" [FULL]

1. **Problem:** DBN post-processing "is inherently bound to fail for several music pieces: pieces with time signature changes, pieces whose tempo falls outside of the tempo range…" (line 34); SOTA (Hung et al. SpecTNT+TCN) is closed-source and unreproduced (line 42).
2. **Method:** 30 s mono @22.05 kHz → 128-bin mel (30 Hz–10 kHz, win 1024, hop 441 → 50 fps), magnitude `ln(1+1000x)`; frontend = stem (per-band BN, 3×4 conv stride to 32 bands ×32 ch, GeLU) + 3 blocks each with a frequency-directed and a time-directed Partial Transformer (Band-Split RoFormer idea), strided conv halving bands / doubling channels; T×4×256 → linear 512; 6 transformer blocks (16 heads ×32, rotary PE, sigmoid gating, FF 2048); two linear heads; **Sum Head** (beat logits = beat + downbeat layers) to force downbeats onto beats (unmatched downbeats >70 ms from a beat fall 1.1 % → 0.62 % on GTZAN). Loss: shift-tolerant weighted BCE `L_st = −Σ_t [ w·y_t·log(m7(ŷ)_t) + (1 − m13(y)_t)·log(1 − m7(ŷ)_t) ]` with `w` = negatives/positives, max-pool 7 frames (±3 = ±70 ms), negatives ignored ±6 frames. Post-processing: peak-pick prob>0.5 within ±3 frames, snap downbeats to nearest beat. Augment: masking 0–6 areas of 0.5–2 s with shuffled sub-parts; ±20/16/12/8/4 % tempo; −5…+6 semitones (22 precomputed variants). Training: 100 epochs, AdamW, lr 8e-4 warm-up 1000 steps, cosine; deliberately continue past rising val-loss to obtain over-confident peaks. ~20 M params (2 M small variant). Datasets: 18 (4,556 tracks), GTZAN test (993). Complexity: dense attention over 1,500 frames per 30 s (flash attention) — O(T²) per block; 8 h on RTX 2080 Ti (lines 46–111).
3. **Results:** Table 2 (GTZAN, mean ± sd over 3 seeds): Beat F1 89.1 ± 0.3 / CMLt 79.8 ± 0.6 / AMLt 89.8 ± 0.4; Downbeat F1 78.3 ± 0.4 / CMLt 67.3 ± 0.8 / AMLt 79.1 ± 0.6; Hung et al. 88.7 / 81.2 / 92.0 and 75.6 / 71.5 / 88.1; small model 88.8 / 79.4 / 89.0 and 77.2 / 65.3 / 78.0; with DBN 88.1 / 80.5 / 91.1 and 77.4 / 73.3 / 87.8. Table 3 ablation (val split, downbeat F1): full 85.4 ± 0.1; no shift tolerance 82.2 ± 0.4; no pitch aug 80.8 ± 0.5; no shift tol. + no weights 68.7 ± 0.8. Table 1 (8-fold): Ballroom beat 97.5, Candombe 99.7, RWC Classical 77.1, SMC 62.7, ASAP downbeat 61.2.
4. **Limitations:** authors — worse CMLt/AMLt (non-periodic beats in hard passages); classical/SMC still weak; annotation quality problems in GTZAN listed by track id; comparison with Hung is indicative only (no reproducible code, unknown split) (lines 105, 119, 135–139). Analyst — 30-s windows concatenated non-overlapping means a boundary artefact every 30 s for a live visualiser; offline model (not causal) — for real-time use a streaming variant (BEAST, ICASSP 2024, cited in S29) or precomputation on the delivered file is required.
5. **Application here:** the mastered file is known BEFORE playback (`render_plan.processed_url`, `measured.duration_seconds`), so beat/downbeat grids can be precomputed server-side once per take (cost amortised over every listener) and shipped as a small JSON alongside `tracks[]`; the visualiser then drives beat-locked events (downbeat = section pulse, beat = particle emission) with zero client CPU. Because CMLt is the weak metric, the DISPLAYED tempo/phase must be smoothed (phase-locked loop or the DBN variant's output for tempo, the DBN-free output for onsets). `tempo_bpm` and `time_signature` from `bindings` give a prior that a DBN could use — this is a BUILD item, not a library call.

### S30 — Palmén, Gilbert, Crossland (Google), "How bold can we be? The impact of adjusting font grade on readability in light and dark polarities", CHI '23 — `[FULL]`, capture `chi23-readability-font-grade-polarity.txt` (64,289 bytes)
1. **Problem, in the authors' framing:** variable-font *grade* (`GRAD`) "achieves boldness without increasing character width or causing text reflow"; the effect of luminance polarity, halation and weight on reading fluency "is not known" (l.7, l.44). Halation is framed as "light spreading beyond its boundaries", the popular argument against dark mode (l.62); grade is the axis proposed to compensate (l.78–80).
2. **Method:** two studies with Roboto Flex. Study 1: glanceable two-word pairs on desktop, 16 px, weight 500, letter-spacing 0.15 px, 184 MTurk participants after a power analysis, 5 grades per mode, repeated measures (l.84, l.95–101). Study 2: paragraph reading on Android phones, 14 px, weight 400, letter-spacing 0.25 px, between-groups grade manipulation; usability-tested first with four usertesting.com panel members (l.84, l.127–129).
3. **Numbers:** Study 1 — significant LM vs DM fluency difference, p=0.004 (l.136); word-pair times DM 2.03 s vs LM 1.92 s at grade 150, LM 1.93 s (p<0.01, d=0.16); "Increasing grade in DM does not bring word-pair reading advantages"; grade significantly affected LM (grade −112.5 → 2.02 s; grade 150 → 1.92 s) (l.199). Study 2 — main effect of mode F=12.8, p<0.01, η²=0.038; LM more readable, t(332)=3.5, p<0.01 (l.201); age effect F=3.0, p=0.007 (l.195); phone-mode self-reports LM 34 % / DM 36 % / switch 25 % (l.189).
4. **Stated limitations:** grade interacts with letter spacing and "smaller letter spacing than those we used could have negative effects on legibility" (l.246); conclusion: at 14 px paragraph reading grade produced no detectable performance or preference difference, which "neither supports nor negates the use of grade to manage the effects of halation" (l.260).
5. **Application to PlayMusicPrompts:** (a) light polarity reads measurably faster in both glanceable and paragraph tasks on emissive screens — this is primary evidence behind the owner's light-surface preference and against a default dark player; (b) if a dark or neon *visualiser* mode exists, the `GRAD` axis of a variable font is the lever to hold apparent weight constant across polarities without reflow, and it costs nothing in paragraph readability at 14 px; (c) glanceable text (track title, timer) is the slower task and is where the mode difference showed most, so the now-playing surface, not the settings page, is the place to spend contrast budget.

### S31 — Erickson et al., "An Extended Analysis on the Benefits of Dark Mode User Interfaces in Optical See-Through Head-Mounted Displays", ACM TAP (extension of SUI 2019) — `[FULL]`, capture `tap21-erickson-dark-mode-ost-hmd.txt` (93,967 bytes)
1. **Problem:** whether light-on-dark AR annotations on OST-HMDs affect visual acuity and fatigue; RQ1 objective acuity benefit, RQ2 preference, RQ3 match between the two (l.44–48).
2. **Method:** 19 participants (10 m / 9 f, ages 18–41, M=25.47, SD=5.93), HoloLens 1, Unity, Landolt-C Golovin-Sivtsev acuity chart, 12 configurations per participant (vision mode × lighting × three printed backgrounds: uniform grey 128, lightness distortions, chromatic), UEQ-S usability, preference forced choice (l.70–127).
3. **Numbers:** acuity rows completed LM M=7.62 (SD 1.31) vs DM M=8.32 (SD 1.27), F(1,18)=9.20, p=0.007, η²p=0.338 — DM higher, **contrary to H1** (l.143, l.163); errors LM 2.52 vs DM 1.10, F(1,18)=12.65, p=0.002 (l.145); clear overall preference for DM, strongest in low-light, balanced in high-light with complex backgrounds (l.175–179).
4. **Stated limitations / mechanism:** the authors attribute the DM advantage to optical scattering in the waveguide (light bleeding from bright glyphs), a property of additive see-through optics (l.167); Erickson's own cross-study comparison notes result variation across OST display types (l.171).
5. **Application:** this is the one rigorous case where dark mode wins, and it is display-physics-specific. For PlayMusicPrompts it bounds the claim "light reads better" to emissive phone/tablet/desktop panels (S30, S19, S20) and tells the visualiser designer that any future AR/glasses companion surface inverts the rule. It also supplies the contradiction ledger entry: S30 and S31 disagree only if the display class is omitted.

### S43 — Zheng, Tahiroğlu, Bryan-Kinns et al. (QMUL C4DM + Aalto), "Exploring gestural affordances in audio latent space navigation", Frontiers in Computer Science, 2025-11-07 — `[FULL]`, capture `frontiers25-qmul-latent-terrain-gestures.txt` (140,859 bytes)
1. **Problem:** how musicians perceive gestural affordances when navigating a neural-audio latent space and turn them into performance technique; prior interfaces reduce dimensionality to sliders/XY-pads and leave the gesture–sound relation under-designed (l.27, l.65, l.83).
2. **Method:** DMI-as-research-probe; stylus-tablet instrument (Wacom Intuos, 21.6×13.5 cm canvas, Bela Trill Bar slider) mapping (x, y, pressure) to an 8-D RAVE latent vector through a fixed "Latent Terrain" generated either by a VAE or a Fourier-CPPN; custom low-complexity guitar-plucking dataset; 6 recurrent 90-minute workshops × 3 musicians = 18 recruited; thematic analysis (l.85, l.97, l.105–117, l.125, l.199).
3. **Numbers:** 18 recruited, 12 completed and consented to analysis, 10 of 12 completed scores for both terrains; 138 minutes of video analysed (l.203). Latent dimensionality 8; input dimensionality 3 (l.97).
4. **Stated limitations:** terrain is fixed after generation (no adaptation to the player) (l.113); the dataset was deliberately simplified to "moderate the amount of surprise" (l.107), so results speak to a constrained timbre space.
5. **Application:** this is the strongest current evidence for a *continuous 2-D + pressure surface* as the control idiom for generative sound, the exact opposite of a parameter form. For PlayMusicPrompts' 101-parameter request body (docs/api/04) the design implication is a two-layer control model: a terrain-like continuous surface for the taste-shaped parameters (mood, energy, brightness) in the foreground and the typed/enforced parameters in a disclosed layer. Direct bridge to S57's Lyria RT controls (brightness, density, tempo, key), which are exactly the kind of low-dimensional descriptors a terrain can carry.

### S51 — Pinder, Odom, Yoo, Misra, Lin, Neustaedter, Barnett (SFU), "Queue Player: Investigating Distributed Co-Listening Experiences for Social Connection across Space, Time, and Tempo", CHI '25 — `[FULL]`, capture `chi25-queue-player-sfu.txt` (122,727 bytes, CC BY 4.0 per l.42)
1. **Problem:** listening histories are large personal archives; *tempo* as an input modality for navigating them is "underexplored in HCI research … and unutilized in current music-listening technologies" (l.77).
2. **Method:** Research-through-Design; four networked domestic players combining four close friends' Spotify histories; tap-tempo selects songs by BPM from the shared archive; Raspberry Pi 3B+, HiFiBerry DAC+, NeoPixel strip (queue) and ring light (current tempo); "slow technology" constraints — songs play to completion, one tempo addition per user until it plays (l.48, l.99–101, l.109–119); six-week field deployment with the four friends in separate homes (l.48).
3. **Numbers:** 4 devices, 4 participants, 6 weeks; qualitative findings in five themes — early disclosure anxiety, re-encountering history (nostalgia), social understanding through slowness, novel everyday uses, adjusting to slow technology (l.139–169).
4. **Stated limitations:** the authors call for study with different "numbers and configurations of friends and loved ones in future research" (l.101); single friend group, RtD not controlled.
5. **Application:** two direct transfers. (a) *Tempo as a first-class navigation control* — the API already returns `measured` tempo per track (docs/api/06); a tap-tempo or BPM-scrub affordance over the user's own library/queue is a documented, novel, tested idiom no mainstream player ships. (b) *Explicit slowness as a design quality* — the queue that refuses to be skipped produced anticipation and intimacy, which argues for a deliberate "listen-through" mode as one of the three structural directions rather than universal skip-everything.

### S57 — Google DeepMind, "Live Music Models" (Magenta RealTime open weights; Lyria RealTime API), NeurIPS 2025 Creative AI track — `[FULL]`, capture `neurips25-live-music-models-lyria-rt.txt` (92,778 bytes)
1. **Problem:** existing generative models produce music "as a noun"; live music models produce "a continuous stream of music in real-time with synchronized user control" (l.7, l.11).
2. **Method:** three-component pipeline — MusicCoCa style embedding, SpectroStream 48 kHz stereo codec, encoder-decoder T5X Transformer — generating two-second chunks with a ten-second audio context (l.44–52, l.76); training set ≈190,000 hours of primarily instrumental stock music, 12-second clips (l.86); inference CFG weight 5.0, temperature 1.3, top-K 40 (l.88); Lyria RT adds MIR-derived controls with self-conditioning control priors (Appendix D, l.415–417, Fig. 5 l.472); audio injection mixes the user's live audio into the next chunk's context (l.127, l.529).
3. **Numbers:** Magenta RT 760 M params, 48 kHz, FD_openl3 72.14 / KL_passt 0.47 / CLAP 0.35 vs Stable Audio Open (1.1 B) 96.51 / 0.55 / 0.41 and MusicGen-stereo-large (3.3 B) 190.47 / 0.52 / 0.31 (l.84); pretrained Base 220 M and Large 770 M (l.86). Table 3 controls: Brightness (log-mel spectral centroid/bandwidth), Density (onset detection), Key (chroma), Tempo (beat prediction model), Stems on/off (Bass, Drums, Vocals, Other) (Appendix D). Table 2: Magenta RT 16 RVQ levels / MusicCoCa vs Lyria RT 64 RVQ levels / MuLan + refinement model + advanced controls + latent constraints. MusicFX DJ named as the first Lyria RT deployment (l.210).
4. **Stated limitations (Appendix B, l.409):** prompt changes "may take two or more seconds to influence the musical output" because of the 2-s chunk; the 10-s context means the model "cannot directly reference music that has been output further into the past" (no long-form structure recall).
5. **Application:** (a) any "live" or "adaptive" mode in PlayMusicPrompts must design for a **≥2 s control-to-sound latency** — the UI needs a committed-change indicator and pre-visualisation of the pending state, or the user will double-act; (b) Lyria RT's exact control vocabulary (brightness, density, key, tempo, stems mute) is the descriptor set an in-house continuous surface (S43) should expose, and each is a measurable feature the response envelope's `measured` block can also report for rendered tracks; (c) the 10-s memory limit means song-form (verse/chorus) must be enforced by the client-side plan (`mix_plan`/`render_plan` in docs/api/06), not expected from the model.

### S58 — Woods et al., "Rapid modulation in music supports attention in listeners with attentional difficulties", Communications Biology 2024 (Brain.fm) — `[FULL]`, capture `commsbio24-woods-rapid-modulation.txt` (73,813 bytes)
1. **Problem:** which musical properties aid sustained attention, given inter-individual variability; hypothesis from optimal-stimulation theory that people with attentional difficulty benefit from more arousing stimulation (l.5, l.17).
2. **Method:** SART (respond to digits, withhold on 10 % targets); three conditions — AM+Music (fast amplitude modulation added), Control−Music (slow modulation), Pink Noise; Exp 1 online (MTurk), Exp 2 fMRI, Exp 3 EEG with stimulus–brain phase-locking, Exp 4 parametric rate and depth manipulation with ASRS split (l.19–21, l.43–45, l.63–65, l.87–95).
3. **Numbers:** Exp 1A N=62 ratings, Exp 1B N=83 SART; fast modulation benefited the *first* block in particular (l.35–41). Exp 2 N=34: higher activation for AM+Music, p<0.05 FDR (l.45–47). Exp 3 N=40: PLV peaks at 8, 12, 14, 16, 24, 32 Hz for AM+Music at 120 bpm; 8 Hz strongest frontally (l.65–75). Exp 4: rate study N=82 (46 low-ASRS, 36 high-ASRS), depth study N=93; three-way interaction F(1,80)=4.03, p=0.048, η²p=0.048 — quadratic rate effect peaking at **16 Hz**, early-block advantage, and dependence on ASRS (l.113–115).
4. **Stated limitations:** effects are early-block and modulated by individual attentional difficulty; the interaction p-value is at the threshold (0.048); modulation added to stock music, not to arbitrary user tracks.
5. **Application:** this supersedes the earlier `[ABS]`-level Brain.fm statement in the main body. For PlayMusicPrompts a "focus" render mode is evidence-backed only if it (a) applies a *16 Hz-region* amplitude modulation, (b) is offered per user with an attentional-difficulty self-report rather than as a universal default, and (c) is presented honestly as "supports attention in listeners with attentional difficulties" — the paper's own title — not as a universal productivity claim. The UI consequence is a consented, per-person mode toggle with the effect described in the product's voice, never a marketing overclaim (owner rule 08 item 46).
