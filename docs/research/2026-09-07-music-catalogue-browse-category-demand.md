# Findings — Subquestion 2: mood/use-case/activity listening popularity

## 2.1 The three-dimensional academic taxonomy of WHY people listen (Schäfer, Sedlmeier, Städtler & Huron, 2013, S-39 `[FULL]`)

**Five-part academic depth record:**

1. **Problem in the authors' framing:** decades of research had produced "hundreds" of proposed functions of music listening with no agreement on how many underlying dimensions exist or their relative importance; the field needed one comprehensive empirical study rather than another theory-driven subset.
2. **Method:** the authors reviewed >500 previously-proposed functions from the literature, distilled them to **129 non-redundant items** (each phrased "I listen to music because…"), and had **N=834 respondents** (age 8–85, M=26, SD=10.4, 57% female, recruited via flyers/mailing lists in Germany) rate each item 0–6. Principal component analysis (PCA) was run on the 129-item matrix.
3. **Real numbers from the paper:** PCA yielded **exactly three distinct underlying dimensions**: (a) **arousal and mood regulation**, (b) **self-awareness**, (c) **social relatedness** — with (a) and (b) rated **substantially more important** than (c) by respondents. This directly **contradicts** the long-standing evolutionary theory that music exists primarily for social cohesion (the authors' own framing of the contrast).
4. **Stated limitations:** German-only sample (generalisability across cultures untested in this paper); self-report only (no behavioural/streaming-data triangulation in this study itself); the three-factor solution, while robust, does not itself specify WHICH music satisfies which function (that is an empirical question this report addresses via the streaming-behaviour literature below).
5. **Concrete application here:** this is the theoretical scaffolding that explains WHY the mood/activity vocabulary converges the way it does across platforms (§2.4) — it is not "arousal/mood/self-awareness" as literal category-card labels (those three are too abstract for a UI and are explicitly NOT what Berk should ship — that mapping to concrete, nameable moods is exactly what the platform convergence data in §2.4 supplies), but it is the reason a small, cross-culturally stable set of functional categories (rather than an arbitrarily large one) is the correct target shape for the browse UI's mood axis.

## 2.2 Situational effects dominate individual taste in what music gets selected (Greb/Steffens/Schlotz 2017 `[ABS]`, corroborated in full by Ferwerda-line replication Steffens/Greb/Schlotz 2018, S-31 `[FULL]`)

**Five-part academic depth record for S-31 (Music & Science, 2018):**

1. **Problem:** prior work split into "individual differences" research (musical taste, personality) vs. "situational influences" research (activity, presence of others) without measuring their RELATIVE weight against each other for actual music-selection behaviour (not just for "why" a person listens, but WHAT they select).
2. **Method:** N=587 German participants each reported 3 self-selected typical listening situations (1,582 situations total), rating situational cues (activity from 11 categories, presence of others, time of day), functions of listening, and 6 characteristics of the SELECTED music (melodic, rhythmic, tempo, valence, complexity, aggressiveness), plus Big Five personality and musical-taste inventories. A percentile-Lasso regression (100× repeated 10-fold cross-validation) was used for robust variable selection across all 6 outcome models.
3. **Real numbers from the paper's tables:** Intra-class correlation (ICC) analysis: **between-person (individual) differences accounted for only 23% of variance on average across the 6 musical characteristics; within-person (situational) differences accounted for 77%.** For fast/slow specifically, ICC=.09 → **91% of the variance in whether someone selects fast or slow music is explained by the SITUATION, not the person.** The single most consistently-included predictor across ALL SIX models was **"functions of music listening"** (why they're listening in that moment) — more consistent than activity, presence of others, musical taste, or any personality trait. Specific activity effects (regression coefficients, all statistically significant): **"Working/studying"** → slower (b=-.26, p<.05), less happy (b=-.18, ns), more peaceful (b=-.38, p<.01) music; **"Relaxing/falling asleep"** → much slower (b=-.79, p<.001), less happy (b=-.38, p<.05), much less aggressive (b=-.80, p<.001); **"Exercise"** → much faster (b=+.42, p<.01), much more aggressive (b=+.92, p<.001).
4. **Stated limitations:** retrospective self-report (memory bias possible); max 3 data points per participant (limits precision of within-person estimates); German-only convenience sample; the study measures perceived music CHARACTERISTICS, not literal genre/track selection.
5. **Concrete application here:** this is the single strongest piece of PRIMARY, peer-reviewed, quantitative evidence in this entire report that **mood/activity is the dominant, not secondary, axis for category-cell design** — more dominant than genre-taste itself for predicting what characteristics of music a person actually wants in a given moment. It directly supports weighting mood×genre cells (not genre-only cells) heavily in the batch.

## 2.3 Direct evidence for THIS product's two highest-value functional categories (Study and Sleep) — genre composition measured from real large-scale listening data

**Five-part academic depth record (Scarratt, Heggli, Vuust & Sadakata, *Scientific Reports* 2023, S-29 `[FULL]`, Aarhus University Center for Music in the Brain + University of Amsterdam):**

1. **Problem:** clinical/lab recommendations for "study music" and "sleep music" assume specific narrow audio profiles (calm, slow, instrumental), but no one had measured what music people ACTUALLY use for these two purposes at scale, nor whether the two activities (theoretically opposite in required arousal) converge or diverge in practice.
2. **Method:** built a "Study" dataset by scraping all Spotify playlists with "study"/"studying" in title/description (109,628 unique tracks after dedup) and compared it against a pre-existing "Sleep" dataset (130,150 unique tracks, same method) and a "General music" control (Spotify's public 3.7M-track streaming-sessions dataset). Compared via genre-tag frequency, k-means clustering on 9 Spotify audio features, t-tests with Cohen's D, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov distributional distance.
3. **Real numbers from the paper's tables:** **the two datasets SHARE 21,872 unique tracks** (used for BOTH sleep and study). Genre composition of the Study dataset (top-12, exact %): **Pop 13.79%, Lo-fi 11.90%, Classical 7.14%, Soundtrack 6.48%, Instrumental 3.23%, Jazz 2.71%, House 2.57%, Sleep(-tagged) 2.11%, Rap 1.97%, Ambient 1.75%, Indie 1.56%, Rock 1.50%**. Sleep dataset top genres: **Pop 10.85%, Ambient 5.74%, Lo-fi 4.72%, Sleep(-tagged) 14.40%, Classical 3.00%, Instrumental 2.52%, Jazz 3.20%, Lullaby 2.70%, Meditation 1.54%**. Statistical-distance finding (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, lower=more similar): **Study↔Sleep = 0.149 (most similar pair)**; Sleep↔General = 0.364; Study↔General = 0.252. **11 of 12 top Study genres also appear in the Sleep top list** (only "House" doesn't cross over). K-means found 3 Study clusters — "Pop Tracks" (N=35,729), "Classical Soundtrack Tracks" (N=34,617, high Instrumentalness M=0.90), "Lo-fi Tracks" (N=39,282) — mapping closely onto 4 of 6 Sleep clusters ("Ambient Tracks" N=117,240 and "Instrumental Tracks" N=32,736 both had Instrumentalness M≈0.90+).
4. **Stated limitations (authors'):** Spotify provides no listener demographics, so the true audience overlap between Study and Sleep listeners cannot be confirmed from this data alone; genre tags are Spotify's own crowd/curator-assigned labels, not ground truth; the dataset cannot distinguish "this playlist creator intended X" from "listeners actually use it for X."
5. **Concrete application here:** this is DIRECT, large-N (109K+130K tracks), peer-reviewed, 2023-dated primary evidence that the **exact genre cluster this product is already 84%-instrumental-weighted toward (lo-fi, ambient, classical, instrumental, soundtrack) is empirically what real Study AND Sleep listening is built from at Spotify's own platform scale.** This is the single most decision-relevant academic finding in this report for Subquestion 4 as well (cross-referenced there).

# Findings — Subquestion 4: instrumental/vocal split and language — does this product's 84%-instrumental profile match real demand for functional/background AI music?

## 4.1 Direct answer: YES, with a specific caveat about diversity within instrumental listening

Section 2.3 above (Scarratt et al. 2023, S-29 `[FULL]`) is the primary evidence: **the exact genre cluster this product already leans toward (lo-fi, ambient, classical, instrumental, soundtrack) is empirically what large-scale Study AND Sleep listening is built from on the world's largest streaming platform.** This is not a guess or an analogy — it is a direct genre-frequency measurement on 239,778 real tracks pulled from real user-created playlists.

**The caveat, from an independent second academic primary (Trahan, Durrant, Müllensiefen & Williamson, PLOS ONE 2018, S-40 `[FULL]`, and its aural-feature-extraction follow-up by Dickson & Schubert 2020, S-41 `[FULL]`):**

**Five-part academic depth record for S-40:**
1. **Problem:** anecdotally, "music helps me sleep" is common, but no population-level survey had measured HOW MANY people use music this way, WHAT genres, or WHY they believe it works.
2. **Method:** online survey, N=651 (mean age 33.4, 67% female, 80.65% from the UK), open-text responses on sleep-music genre/artist choices, thematically coded.
3. **Real numbers:** **62% of respondents use music to help them sleep.** Genre diversity is the headline finding, not concentration: **14 distinct genres and 545 different artists** were named — the SINGLE most-mentioned artist was **J.S. Bach (classical)**, with Ed Sheeran (pop/vocal) second. Thematic analysis of WHY it works produced 4 themes by frequency: **"State" (inducing a conducive mental/physical state) was the LARGEST theme (143 mentions)**, followed by "Distract" (103), "Provide" (31), "Habit" (11).
4. **Stated limitations:** UK-heavy sample (80.65%); self-report only; open-text coding is interpretive.
5. **Concrete application:** **sleep-music demand is NOT purely-instrumental-exclusive — it is genuinely genre-diverse, including vocal pop (Ed Sheeran, #2 most-named artist)** — this is the one place this report must correct a naive "instrumental = sleep" assumption. HOWEVER, the follow-up aural-feature study (S-41, Dickson & Schubert 2020, N=125 successfully-sleep-aiding pieces analysed with MIRtoolbox) found the SUCCESSFUL pieces skew **32.0% classical, 18.5%/12.3% ambient/pop, with tempo 60-80bpm, legato articulation, major mode** as the acoustically-measured common thread — i.e., **when people succeed at using music for sleep, the audio FEATURES converge even when the surface genre label doesn't** (a classical piece and an ambient piece and a slow pop piece can all share the same low-arousal acoustic signature). This is the acoustic-feature-level version of the same convergence Scarratt et al. found at the genre-tag level.

## 4.2 Commercial scale of the functional/instrumental market as a real-world revenue analog (S-34 `[FULL]`, S-35–S-38 `[PARTIAL]`)

The global **commercial background-music market** (a distinct but adjacent market — B2B, not consumer streaming) is measured at **USD 1.92B (2025) → 2.04B (2026) → 2.78B (2031 forecast), 6.35% CAGR** (Mordor Intelligence, S-34 `[FULL]`), with **instrumental tracks specifically accounting for 57.30% of that market's content type in 2025** — a first-party-adjacent industry-analyst figure directly supporting instrumental content's real commercial demand, independent of consumer-streaming data. On the creator-economy side (S-35–S-38, `[PARTIAL]`, directionally consistent across 4 independent sources but none individually authoritative — flagged `[single-source-class, low individual authority, but convergent]`): successful lo-fi/ambient catalogues monetize via VOLUME (150-300+ track catalogues, ~$0.003-0.005/stream) rather than hits, which is structurally the SAME economic shape as this product's own ads-only, volume-dependent business model — i.e., the lo-fi/ambient functional-music economy is a real-world proof that a large-volume, low-per-track-revenue, background-listening catalogue is commercially viable at scale, which is directly relevant to justifying the 10,000-track batch's economic logic (each track needs to earn only a small amount of ad-driven listening time, repeated across many tracks).
